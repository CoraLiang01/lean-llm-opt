\textbf{\large Response to the Associate Editor: within-category heterogeneity}

\textit{Structural results verified on 6 October 2026.}

\textbf{Comment.} \textit{The second question concerns the heterogeneity of
problems even within each category. In my opinion, the reason that the
proposed method performed quite well may be attributed to the close
resemblance between the reference problem and the actual problem. However,
in reality, even when two problems belong to the same category, they can
still be drastically different. For example, there could be many additional
constraints, varying objectives, or other business requirements one may
consider in one type of problem. Would the current approach be robust
facing such conditions? How well can it perform? I didn't see clear evidence
from the current paper. I hope that the authors can also perform some tests
to analyze such scenarios.}

\begingroup\color{replyblue}
\textbf{Response.} We agree that sharing a problem category does not imply
sharing a formulation. We added a formulation-level structural comparison
and complementary performance tests to examine reference dependence.

\medskip
\textbf{Motivation.}
Semantic similarity alone does not reveal which variables and constraints
interact. We therefore combine the variable--constraint bipartite
representation used in MILP research \citep{Gasse2019} with the WL subtree
kernel \citep{Shervashidze2011}. Related work also uses graph-based MILP
similarity for solver configuration \citep{Hosny2024}; our diagnostic uses
an untrained, auditable feature map.

\medskip
\textbf{Method.}
We compare 101 Large-Scale-OR formulations and 36 task variants with
15 current reference formulations. To remove arbitrary differences in
bound storage, we convert singleton rows to bounds and intersect them
with native bounds. We then use two conventions: (i) store effective
bounds in \texttt{Bounds} without graph nodes; (ii) represent each finite
lower and upper bound as an explicit inequality node, including default
nonnegativity and binary $0/1$ bounds. Variable domains and remaining
rows are retained, with no general presolving or variable elimination.
The conversions were checked against the original formulations.

Each variable and retained constraint becomes a node, joined whenever
its matrix coefficient is nonzero. Initial labels distinguish binary,
integer, and continuous variables, and equality versus inequality rows.
Coefficient values and signs, objective terms, right-hand-side values,
bound values, and names are omitted.

Following \citet{Shervashidze2011}, initialize $l_0(v)=\ell(v)$ and update
synchronously with a dictionary shared across graphs:
\[
\begin{aligned}
M_i(v)&=\{\!\{l_{i-1}(u):u\in\mathcal N(v)\}\!\},\\
s_i(v)&=\operatorname{concat}
 \big(l_{i-1}(v),\operatorname{sort}(M_i(v))\big),\qquad
l_i(v)=f(s_i(v)).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $\mathcal N(v)$ denotes neighbors, double braces retain repeated
labels, and $f$ assigns the same label exactly when signatures match.
Let $\Sigma_i$ be the shared set of labels at round $i$, and let
$c_i(G,\sigma)$ denote the number of nodes in graph $G$ with label
$\sigma$ at that round. We concatenate these counts from rounds
$0$--$2$ into $\phi^{(2)}_{\WL}(G)$, using the same coordinate order
across graphs and separate coordinates for different rounds.
A label absent from a graph contributes a zero count.

The WL subtree kernel compares the two graphs by multiplying counts
of the same label at the same round and summing these products across
rounds $0$--$2$:
\[
\left\langle\phi^{(2)}_{\WL}(G_P),\phi^{(2)}_{\WL}(G_Q)\right\rangle
=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_i}
c_i(G_P,\sigma)c_i(G_Q,\sigma).
\]
Since this inner product depends on the magnitude of the count vectors,
we normalize it by their Euclidean norms to obtain cosine similarity:
\[
\begin{aligned}
S_2(P,Q)
&=\frac{\langle\phi^{(2)}_{\WL}(G_P),\phi^{(2)}_{\WL}(G_Q)\rangle}
{\|\phi^{(2)}_{\WL}(G_P)\|\,\|\phi^{(2)}_{\WL}(G_Q)\|}\\
&=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_i}
c_i(G_P,\sigma)c_i(G_Q,\sigma)}
{\displaystyle\sqrt{
\left(\sum_{i=0}^{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_i}c_i(G_P,\sigma)^2\right)
\left(\sum_{i=0}^{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_i}c_i(G_Q,\sigma)^2\right)}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Each norm is the square root of the sum of squared counts.
The score lies in $[0,1]$, with larger values indicating closer
cumulative label-count representations. Normalization removes dependence
on overall vector magnitude, but does not eliminate structural changes
associated with graph size. All three rounds contribute to the score;
round two is not used in isolation. The graph encoding and cosine
normalization are our application choices.

For each test formulation $P$, let $g(P)$ denote its category,
$\mathcal R_{g(P)}$ its same-category reference pool, and $\mathcal R$
the full reference collection. We report the nearest-reference scores
\[
S_{\rm same}(P)=\max_{R\in\mathcal R_{g(P)}}S_2(P,R),
\qquad
S_{\rm all}(P)=\max_{R\in\mathcal R}S_2(P,R).
\]
We then compute category-level medians and means of these per-instance
maxima. There is one reference each for NRM, RA, TP, AP, and UFLP,
eight for Mixture, and two for Others; the Mixture and Others pools
remain separate. We retain the existing $h=2$ setting and report both
bound conventions.

\medskip
\textbf{Interpretation and implications.}
The results show category-dependent resemblance rather than uniform
identity to the reference pool. Including common bound rows increases
the reported medians on these data, demonstrating representation
sensitivity. Allowing cross-category matches also raises some scores:
AP's median rises from $0.0508$ to $0.4424$ with bounds excluded.
A difference from a same-category reference therefore does not establish
a difference from every available reference. Scores are not overlap
percentages, and no universal high/low threshold is assumed.

Two cases require particular caution. AP's continuous test variables
differ from its binary reference encoding. NRM's low scores largely
reflect a synthetic shared-dispatch row previously added to its reference
after inspecting resemblance; removing that row from a diagnostic copy
restores all 25 scores to $1$. Neither finding independently establishes
novel business requirements. The variant set also has a different
category composition, so pooled medians do not establish that each
variant is less similar than its original problem.

The WL analysis addresses selected structural resemblance, not robustness
to omitted numerical or objective changes. Complementary saved evaluations
matched reference objectives for 29/36 variants ($80.56\%$), 88/101
instances under LOTO example-and-route withholding ($87.13\%$, versus
$94.06\%$ for its full baseline), and 431/505 non-designated forced-route
assignments ($85.35\%$). Failures remain in the denominators; the 505
assignments concern repeated routes on 101 problems. These evaluations
use their respective saved implementation snapshots and should not be
pooled as a common-version comparison. Together, they provide evidence
of resilience within the tested conditions, while neither WL scores nor
objective matching prove full formulation correctness or universal
generalization to arbitrary new modeling requirements.
